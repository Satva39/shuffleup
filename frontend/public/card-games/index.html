Trick-taking · 4–10 players

Kachuful

Kachuful is a trick-taking card game where every player predicts how many tricks they will win in a round. ShuffleUp implements a variable-round version: hands grow from one card per player up to the largest deal supported by the player count.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · 3–6 players

Teen Patti

ShuffleUp’s Teen Patti is a three-card comparison game played over a series of rounds. This implementation uses an in-game points system; it does not include betting, chips or real-money wagering.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · 2–6 players

Indian Rummy

Indian Rummy is a hand-building card game. In ShuffleUp, each player starts with 13 cards and tries to form valid groups before making a declaration. A valid declaration must satisfy the implementation’s sequence requirements.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · 2–12 players

Mangoose

Mangoose is a card-placement and shedding game with personal closed and open piles, plus four central foundations—one for each suit. The goal is to empty your own cards while making legal placements.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Casual · 2–12 players

UNO

UNO is a shedding card game built around matching the active color or number and using action cards at the right moment. ShuffleUp’s implementation supports 2–12 players and carries round scores into a longer match.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · 2–8 players

Jack Thief

Jack Thief is a matching and elimination card game. In the ShuffleUp implementation, pairs of equal rank are removed when possible, players finish as their hands empty, and the final unmatched Jack determines the loser.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Trick-taking · Exactly 5 players

Napoleon

ShuffleUp implements a specific five-player Japanese Napoleon-style trick-taking variant. Its bidding, blind, called partner card and special card ranks are defined by the app’s rules and may differ from other regional versions.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Trick-taking · Exactly 4 players

Bridge

Bridge is a partnership trick-taking card game. ShuffleUp uses four seats—North, East, South and West—with North/South on one team and East/West on the other.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Trick-taking · Exactly 4 players

Spades

Spades is a four-player partnership trick-taking game. North/South form one team and East/West form the other. Spades are always trump in this ShuffleUp implementation.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Trick-taking · Exactly 4 players

Twenty-Nine (29)

Twenty-Nine, also called the 29 card game, is a four-player partnership trick-taking game. ShuffleUp uses a 32-card deck and a bidding phase to set a target for the contract team.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Trick-taking · Exactly 4 players

Mindi Coat

Mindi Coat is a four-player partnership trick-taking game. ShuffleUp includes a hidden Hukum card that can later reveal the trump suit, and hand results depend on captured Tens and tricks.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Casual · 2–6 players

Bluff (Cheat)

Bluff, sometimes called Cheat, is a social deduction card game where players make rank claims about face-down cards. Opponents decide whether to accept the claim or challenge it during the challenge window.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · 3–8 players

Satte Pe Satta

Satte Pe Satta is a sequence-building card game where players extend four suit rows around the sevens. ShuffleUp starts with the 7 of Hearts already placed and uses penalty points based on cards left in players’ hands.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Classic · Exactly 2 players

War

War is a two-player card game built around repeated rank comparisons. Both players reveal a card, and the higher rank wins the battle pile. Ties trigger an additional War resolution.

Read the rules and how-to-play guide → Casual · 2–8 players

Solitaire Multiplayer

ShuffleUp’s multiplayer Solitaire is a competitive Klondike-style game. Each player works on a separate board and separate deck, while the server compares progress. Players do not take turns on one shared tableau.
